Every mechanical watch, from a $100 Seiko to a $1,000,000 grand complication, uses the same five systems.

## 1. Power: the mainspring

A long ribbon of special alloy is coiled inside a drum called the **barrel**. Winding (by the crown or the rotor) tightens the spring. As it slowly uncoils, it turns the barrel, and that is the energy for everything else.

## 2. Transmission: the gear train

The barrel drives a chain of wheels: the **centre wheel** (which turns once an hour and carries the minute hand), the **third wheel**, the **fourth wheel** (once a minute, often carrying the seconds hand) and finally the **escape wheel**. Each step speeds the rotation up and reduces the force.

## 3. Distribution: the escapement

The **escape wheel** would spin freely if nothing stopped it. The **pallet fork** locks it, then releases it one tooth at a time. Each release gives a tiny push, the **impulse**, to the balance. This lock-and-release is the tick.

## 4. Regulation: the balance and hairspring

The **balance wheel** and its spiral **hairspring** form an oscillator, like a pendulum that works in any position. Their natural frequency, for example **28,800 vibrations per hour (4 Hz)**, sets the pace of the whole watch. Shortening or lengthening the active part of the hairspring makes the watch run faster or slower: this is **regulation**.

## 5. Display: the motion works

Under the dial, a small set of gears (the **motion works**) reduces the minute wheel's rotation by 12:1 to drive the hour hand.

## Where automatic winding fits in

An automatic adds a **rotor** and a **reversing or Magic Lever system** that turns the rotor's swings, in either direction, into winding of the mainspring. A **slipping bridle** stops the spring from being over-wound.

## Jewels

Pivots run in synthetic **ruby jewels** to cut friction and wear. A simple three-hand automatic has about 21–25 functional jewels. More jewels do not automatically mean a better watch.
