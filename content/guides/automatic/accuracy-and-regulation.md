A mechanical watch is never perfect. The question is how far from perfect, and how consistently.

## Seconds per day (s/d)

Accuracy is measured as how many seconds a watch gains (+) or loses (−) in 24 hours.

| Standard | Typical spec |
|---|---|
| Entry automatic (e.g. Seiko NH35) | −20 to +40 s/d |
| Swiss "elaboré" grade (e.g. ETA 2824-2) | ±7 s/d average |
| COSC chronometer | −4 to +6 s/d |
| Rolex Superlative Chronometer | −2 to +2 s/d |
| METAS Master Chronometer | 0 to +5 s/d |

Real-world results often beat the specification.

## Why rates vary

- **Position:** gravity pulls on the balance differently when the watch is dial-up, crown-down and so on. Watchmakers test in up to six positions.
- **State of wind:** a fully wound mainspring delivers more force than a nearly empty one. Better movements are more **isochronous**.
- **Temperature:** modern alloys and silicon hairsprings reduce this effect.
- **Magnetism:** the number-one cause of a suddenly fast watch.

## Measuring at home

1. Set the watch exactly against an atomic time source (such as time.is).
2. Check it at the same time each day for a week, noting the difference.
3. Divide the total by the number of days.

A **timegrapher** (or a phone app) measures the **rate**, **amplitude** (the balance's swing, ideally 270–310° when fully wound) and **beat error** in seconds.

## Using positions to your advantage

If your watch runs fast, leave it **crown-up** or **dial-up** overnight, whichever slows it. Many owners "regulate by position" without opening the case.
