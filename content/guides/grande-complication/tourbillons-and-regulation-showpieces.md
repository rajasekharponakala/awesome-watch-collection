## The tourbillon

Patented by **Abraham-Louis Breguet** in 1801, the tourbillon puts the entire escapement and balance in a rotating **cage**, typically turning once a minute. In a pocket watch, which sits vertically for hours, rotation averages out the rate errors that gravity causes in vertical positions.

**On the wrist the benefit is debated**, because a wristwatch changes position constantly. Today the tourbillon is mostly a showcase of skill: the cage of a one-minute tourbillon can weigh less than 0.3 g and contains dozens of parts.

### Variants

- **Flying tourbillon:** supported from one side only, with no top bridge, so it seems to float.
- **Multi-axis tourbillons:** cages rotating in two or three axes (Jaeger-LeCoultre Gyrotourbillon, Greubel Forsey).
- **Carrousel:** similar effect, but driven differently (Bahne Bonniksen, 1892). Blancpain has revived it.

## Fusée-and-chain

A tiny bicycle-like chain wraps around a cone (the fusée). When the spring is fully wound, it pulls on the narrow part of the cone; as it unwinds, the larger diameter compensates for the lower force. The chain of an A. Lange & Söhne Pour le Mérite has more than 600 parts.

## Is it worth it?

As engineering art, absolutely. As a precision device, a modern chronometer-grade movement with a silicon hairspring is often just as accurate. Judge a tourbillon on **finishing, architecture and the maker's skill**. Today's Chinese industrial tourbillons cost a few hundred dollars, while the finest hand-made ones cost hundreds of thousands.
