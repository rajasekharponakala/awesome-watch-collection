The escapement is where watchmaking becomes physics. It has to give the balance just enough energy, at just the right moment, while disturbing it as little as possible.

## The Swiss lever escapement

Derived from **Thomas Mudge's** detached lever (c. 1755). The pallet fork alternately locks and releases the escape wheel; the **impulse** is delivered through **sliding friction** between the escape wheel teeth and the pallet jewels. It is robust, self-starting and cheap to produce, but that sliding requires lubrication, which degrades over time.

## The detent escapement

The **chronometer escapement** of marine clocks. Impulse is given directly to the balance in only one direction, with almost no friction. It is extremely accurate, but sensitive to shocks and not self-starting, so it is rarely used in wristwatches.

## The co-axial escapement

Invented by **George Daniels** in the 1970s and industrialised by **Omega** in 1999. Impulse is delivered radially, through pushing rather than sliding, which reduces friction and the need for lubrication. It is now in nearly all Omega mechanical watches.

## Silicon and new materials

Silicon (Si) parts are light, hard, anti-magnetic and need no lubrication. They can be made with micron precision by **DRIE etching**. Silicon escape wheels, pallet forks and hairsprings (Rolex Syloxi, Omega Si14, Patek Spiromax) have transformed modern accuracy and reliability.

## Constant force

Mainspring torque falls as it unwinds, which affects amplitude and rate. **Constant-force escapements** and **remontoirs** store energy in a small intermediate spring that is rewound at regular intervals, so the balance receives identical impulses. Often a one-second "dead-beat" seconds hand is the visible sign.

## Further experiments

The **Girard-Perregaux Constant Escapement** (a buckling silicon blade), the **Zenith Defy Lab** oscillator (a single silicon piece replacing over 30 parts) and **Ulysse Nardin's Freak** are ongoing attempts to reinvent the oldest problem in horology.
